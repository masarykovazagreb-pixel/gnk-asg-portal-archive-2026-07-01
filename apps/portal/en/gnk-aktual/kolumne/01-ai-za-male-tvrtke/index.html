Artificial Intelligence Isn't a Tool for the Big Players. That's Exactly Why It's Dangerous for Small Ones Who Ignore It

Every week someone asks me whether their company needs artificial intelligence. It's the wrong question. The question isn't whether to use it, but how long your company can afford not to.

The biggest misconception about AI is that it's a tool for large systems with large budgets. In practice it's the opposite. A big company can absorb a year of inefficiency and no one will notice it on the balance sheet. A small company doesn't have that luxury. Every hour spent manually solving a problem that could be automated is an hour its competitor, somewhere else, is no longer spending.

I've seen this up close. Across our group we work with businesses in six different industries — from energy to healthcare — and the pattern is the same everywhere. The companies that reached for automation first weren't the wealthiest. They were the most impatient. An owner who refused to pay for a third employee to do work software does just as well now has a margin his neighborhood competitor can't figure out how to match.

What worries me isn't the speed of adoption — that's, surprisingly, good. What worries me is the quality of understanding. I see companies buy a tool, don't change the process, and then wonder why the results don't show up. AI doesn't fix a bad process. It accelerates it, mistakes included. Automate chaos, and you get faster chaos.

There's an equally common opposite extreme: paralysis in the face of choice. Ten tools, a hundred webinars, not a single decision. Here an old rule of entrepreneurship applies — which tool you pick matters less than picking one and starting to measure. The first month won't be perfect. But a first month with data is worth more than six months of thinking without it.

For small and mid-sized businesses the message is simple, if uncomfortable: this isn't a passing technological fad. It's a shift in the level at which competition happens. Whoever waits for it to become cheaper, safer, or clearer will wait until the gap between them and the competition becomes too large to close.

I'm not an optimist because I believe in technology for its own sake. I'm an optimist because I know how many entrepreneurs in this country are ready to make the difference between surviving and growing — the moment someone shows them that the difference exists.
