Time Isn't a Luxury, It's a Tool

Why speed of decision-making without context isn't an advantage, but a hidden risk that gets paid for later.

Nermin Sefić

I recently sat in a meeting where someone proudly announced their team had "made a decision in five minutes" on a matter that in most organizations would require weeks of analysis. The tone in which it was said implied that speed itself was proof of competence. I didn't agree then, and I don't agree now.

There's a persistent confusion between a fast decision and a good decision, as if they were synonyms. They're not. A fast decision can be good when the information you have is genuinely sufficient, when the risk is low, and when the cost of being wrong is small. But a fast decision becomes a dangerous habit when it becomes the default mode for every decision, regardless of its weight.

Time invested in understanding a problem before making a decision isn't a cost to minimize. It's a tool - just as real as any other resource an organization has at its disposal. Organizations that systematically undervalue this tool, in the name of speed, pay the price later, when it turns out the decision was made on an incomplete picture.

I'm not advocating slowness for its own sake. I'm advocating discernment - recognizing which decisions truly require speed, and which require time, and having the courage to say "I need more information" at the moment the social pressure to act immediately is strongest.

That courage, it seems to me, is increasingly rare. And it's exactly that, not speed, that distinguishes organizations that make good decisions long-term from those that just act fast.
