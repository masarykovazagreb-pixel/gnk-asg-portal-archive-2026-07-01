I Learned to Listen to a Client's Silence

Home›Columns›I Learned to Listen to a Client

A client who stops complaining isn't necessarily a satisfied client - sometimes they've simply given up expecting improvement.

Nermin Sefić

For a long time I misread the absence of complaints as a sign of satisfaction - a client who previously regularly raised concerns, and then stopped, I would interpret as a sign the problems were resolved. Later, through the uncomfortable experience of losing a longtime client, I realized how wrong that assumption could be.

Silence sometimes does mean satisfaction. But just as often it means something entirely opposite - a client who has given up expecting things to improve, and simply stopped investing energy in communicating problems they no longer believe will be resolved. This quiet resignation is considerably more dangerous than a loud complaint, because it stays invisible until the moment the client simply leaves, without the warning a loud complaint would have provided.

Today I actively look for signals of this quiet resignation - a change in the tone of communication, reduced engagement, shorter, more formal responses than once more extensive conversations. These subtle signals, if recognized in time, offer an opportunity for intervention that a loud complaint automatically provides, but quiet resignation deliberately hides until it's too late.
