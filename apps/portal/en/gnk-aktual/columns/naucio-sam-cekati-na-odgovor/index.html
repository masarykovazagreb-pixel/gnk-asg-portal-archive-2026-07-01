I Learned to Wait for an Answer Instead of Giving One

Home›Columns›I Learned to Wait for an Answer Instead of Giving One

The shift from instinctively providing solutions to patiently waiting for the team to arrive at one themselves.

Nermin Sefić

For a long time I considered quickly providing solutions a sign of good leadership - someone raises a problem, I immediately offer an answer, the meeting moves on. Efficient, effective, seemingly useful at first glance. What I recognized too late is that this speed, however useful it seemed in the moment, was preventing the team from developing its own problem-solving capability.

The change came gradually - instead of an instant answer, I started asking the question back: "What do you think we should do?" The first reactions were uncertain, sometimes frustrated - the team was used to me giving answers, not being asked for their own. But over time, I noticed a gradual, but real growth in independence and confidence in their own judgment.

This change required patience that didn't come naturally to me - an answer I could give in a few seconds now requires minutes of waiting while the team arrives at its own solution, sometimes different, sometimes even better than the one I would have immediately offered. But the long-term value of built independence far outweighs the short-term efficiency of quickly giving answers.
