A Mistake Should Be Admitted Faster Than It Was Made

Why the speed of admitting a mistake, not avoiding it, builds real trust.

Nermin Sefić

There's an instinct, almost universal in the business world, to first try to hide or minimize a mistake before admitting it. I understand that instinct - it comes from fear that admitting a mistake will be read as weakness, as proof of incompetence. But my experience shows the opposite: organizations and individuals who quickly, clearly admit mistakes build stronger long-term trust than those who try to hide them.

The reason is simple, though rarely said out loud. Mistakes, in the vast majority of cases, eventually come to light - whether internally, externally, immediately, or years later. The question is never whether a mistake will surface, but when, and who will discover it. An organization that admits its own mistake quickly controls the narrative around it. An organization whose mistake is discovered externally, after an attempt to cover it up, loses both the mistake and trust at the same time.

This isn't a call for unnecessary self-punishment or public flagellation over every small error. It's about distinguishing - there's a difference between transparent, quick admission of a real mistake followed by a concrete correction plan, and paranoid, defensive admission of every trivial thing out of fear of criticism. The first builds trust. The second creates fatigue and cynicism in those listening.

A test I've learned to apply: when I discover a mistake, I ask myself not "how do I minimize this," but "who needs to know, and how quickly can I tell them." That simple shift in question changes the entire dynamic - instead of defense, comes accountability. And accountability, consistently demonstrated over time, is what ultimately builds what we're all looking for when we say "trust."
