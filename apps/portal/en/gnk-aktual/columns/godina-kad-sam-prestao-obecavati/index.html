The Year I Stopped Making Promises I Couldn't Control

Home›Columns›The Year I Stopped Making Promises I Couldn&#x27;t Control

The difference between promising your own effort and promising an outcome that depends on factors beyond your control.

Nermin Sefić

For a long time, with good intentions, I made promises I couldn't actually control in reality - "this will definitely work," "by the end of the year we'll be where we want to be." These promises came from honest optimism and a wish to encourage the team, but they carried a hidden cost I recognized too late - when an outcome, dependent on numerous factors outside my control, didn't turn out as I'd promised, trust would be damaged, regardless of how much effort I'd put in.

The change I made: I stopped promising outcomes, and started promising only what I could genuinely control - my own effort, transparency about progress, speed of response to changing circumstances. "I promise we'll give it our best and be transparent about the obstacles we encounter" sounds less inspiring than "this will definitely work," but it carries an integrity the latter simply can't offer, because the latter promises something not fully within my power to guarantee.

This change required accepting discomfort - people sometimes want to hear confident promises, not nuanced statements about effort and transparency. But in the long run, this more honest communication builds deeper, more resilient trust than any number of confident promises which, statistically speaking, will inevitably sometimes fail to materialize despite the best intentions and greatest effort.
