The Silence After a Question

Why the rush to fill silence after asking a question is often more costly than the silence itself.

Nermin Sefić

I learned this relatively late in my career: when I ask a hard question in a meeting, and no one answers immediately, the worst thing I can do is fill that silence with my own answer or assumption. The instinct is nearly irresistible - silence is uncomfortable, and filling it is easy, especially when you're the person who asked the question.

But that silence, however uncomfortable, is often the most productive moment of the entire meeting. It's the moment when people actually think, when an answer forms that isn't just the first thing that comes to mind, but a genuinely considered response. Interrupting that silence with my own voice, even with the good intention of "helping" the conversation along, often means stealing exactly the moment of reflection the question was meant to trigger.

This is especially hard for people in leadership positions, where there's an added layer of pressure - silence can feel like a sign you're not in control, that the meeting "isn't going" as it should. The reality is the opposite. The ability to sit in silence, to let others have space to think before answering, is one of the most powerful, if least visible, tools of good leadership.

Next time you ask a question and feel that uncomfortable silence that follows, try counting to at least ten before speaking. You'll be surprised how often someone else steps in with an answer worth the wait - an answer that wouldn't exist if you'd interrupted the silence too soon.
