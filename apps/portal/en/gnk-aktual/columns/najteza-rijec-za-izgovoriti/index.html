The Hardest Word to Say

Why the word "sorry," said honestly and unconditionally, is harder, but more valuable, than any sophisticated apology.

Nermin Sefić

There's a difference I overlooked for a long time between "I'm sorry that happened" and simply "I'm sorry, I made a mistake." The first phrasing, though it sounds similar, actually avoids taking responsibility - it speaks of regret over an outcome, not acknowledgment of one's own error that led to that outcome.

This subtle but significant difference becomes obvious when you listen carefully to how people, including me in the past, phrase apologies. "I'm sorry if you felt hurt" shifts the focus to the other person's reaction, implicitly suggesting the problem lies in perception, not in one's own action. "I'm sorry, I made a mistake" directly takes responsibility, without qualification or shifting focus.

I've learned, through an uncomfortable but valuable experience, that the second phrasing - harder to say precisely because it leaves no room for defense or nuance - builds significantly deeper trust than the first. People recognize the difference, even when they can't immediately articulate it, between an apology that genuinely takes responsibility and one that only performatively mimics it while actually avoiding real acknowledgment.
