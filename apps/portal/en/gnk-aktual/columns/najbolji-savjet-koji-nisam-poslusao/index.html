The Best Advice I Didn't Follow

The difference between good advice and advice that's actually applicable in the specific moment it's given.

Nermin Sefić

A few years ago, someone whose opinion I value told me to slow down - to stop trying to solve every problem immediately, and learn to wait for the right moment. It was good advice. I didn't follow it, at least not right away, and that decision cost me time and energy I later had to spend fixing the consequences of decisions made too quickly.

It's interesting to think about why I ignored advice that, even at the moment it was given, I knew was probably correct. The answer, I think, lies in the difference between intellectually agreeing with advice and actually being willing to apply it at the moment the pressure is greatest. It's easy to nod along when someone says "slow down" during a calm conversation. It's considerably harder to actually slow down when a deadline is close, when everyone around you seems to be moving fast, and when slowness feels like weakness.

This distinction - between knowing and applying - seems underrated to me in most discussions about leadership. We focus on giving good advice, as if the biggest challenge is arriving at the right insight. The real challenge, at least in my experience, often lies elsewhere - in finding the courage and discipline to actually apply that already-known, already-accepted insight at the moment it's hardest to do so.

Today, when I give advice to others, I try to remember this lesson. It's not enough to tell someone what they should do. The more valuable conversation is often the one that explores why it will likely be hard to actually apply it - and what might help so that, this time, unlike me back then, the advice is actually followed when it's needed most.
