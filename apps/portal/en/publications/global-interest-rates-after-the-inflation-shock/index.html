Global Interest Rates After the Inflation Shock

How a shift in the monetary cycle affects the cost of capital, refinancing, and investment decisions of international groups.

How a shift in the monetary cycle affects the cost of capital, refinancing, and investment decisions of international groups.

Monetary policy is no longer a background variable. For international business groups, it directly determines the cost of debt, asset value, and the profitability threshold of new investments.

The greatest risk isn't the interest rate level itself, but the wrong assumption that the cost of capital will quickly return to previous levels. Management must therefore plan multiple refinancing scenarios, shorter decision deadlines, and a higher liquidity reserve.

Capital discipline means linking the investment plan, cash flow, currency exposure, and a project's real ability to withstand more expensive financing. Growth without such control creates an illusion of value while increasing systemic risk.

GNK ASG approaches financial management through documented assumptions, accountability for decisions, and measurable risk thresholds.

The interest rate environment following recent inflation shocks differs meaningfully from the low-rate conditions that shaped financial planning assumptions for much of the previous decade.

Organisations that have not revisited capital structure assumptions built during the low-rate era risk carrying financing costs poorly matched to current market conditions.

This reassessment extends beyond debt financing to affect discount rates used in investment appraisal, pension fund assumptions, and working capital management practices across the organisation.

Stress-testing existing debt structures against a range of plausible future rate scenarios reveals refinancing risk that static assumptions built in a different rate environment would obscure.

Organisations that delay this reassessment risk discovering the mismatch only when refinancing becomes urgent rather than optional.
