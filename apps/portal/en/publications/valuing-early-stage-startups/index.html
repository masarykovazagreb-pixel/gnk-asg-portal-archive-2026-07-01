Valuing a pre-revenue startup relies on assumptions the investor and founder must explicitly align on before negotiating price.

Standard discounted-cash-flow valuation methods are hard to apply to a startup with no revenue history, so the valuation often becomes a negotiation over assumptions, not a calculation.

Comparison with recent transactions of similar companies (comparable transactions) offers a reference point, but requires careful adjustment for differences in market, team, and product-development stage.

The investment structure — convertible note, SAFE, or a direct equity purchase — affects the real cost of capital just as much as the nominal valuation, a difference often underestimated.

Clearly documenting the assumptions behind a valuation, including expected growth and comparable transactions, eases future financing rounds and reduces room for later dispute.

Valuation approaches appropriate for established businesses often translate poorly to early-stage startups, where traditional metrics may not yet meaningfully reflect the venture's actual prospects.

Valuation approaches that weight team quality and market opportunity appropriately, alongside limited financial history, tend to produce more useful assessments than purely financial models.

Investors and acquirers who apply purely traditional valuation frameworks to early-stage ventures risk both overpaying and underpaying, depending on which metrics happen to dominate the analysis.

Investors and acquirers who develop genuine expertise in early-stage valuation methodologies, distinct from frameworks designed for established businesses, make more informed decisions than those applying familiar but poorly suited traditional approaches. This specialised expertise becomes increasingly valuable as early-stage investment activity continues to represent a significant and growing share of overall capital deployment.
