Currency Hedging for Exporters

An exporter who doesn't hedge currency risk is actually taking a speculative position, even when that decision was never consciously made.

Nermin Sefić2 August 2026

An exporter who doesn't hedge currency risk is actually taking a speculative position, even when that decision was never consciously made.

A company invoicing in foreign currency while incurring costs in domestic currency automatically carries currency risk regardless of whether it has ever formally analysed or decided to manage it.

Indirect hedging through natural alignment of revenue and costs in the same currency is often cheaper than financial instruments, but requires a deliberate contract structure, not coincidence.

When natural hedging isn't sufficient, forward contracts and currency options only make sense with a clearly defined policy — what percentage of exposure is covered, over what term, and with what approval.

The absence of a written currency policy doesn't mean the absence of risk; it just means no one is formally deciding on it.

Consistent application of a documented hedging policy, rather than adjusting positions based on the prevailing mood about exchange rate direction, tends to produce better long-term outcomes even when any single hedge appears suboptimal in hindsight.

Reviewing hedge effectiveness periodically against the policy's original objectives, not just against realised gains or losses, keeps the programme aligned with its actual purpose of reducing volatility rather than speculating on currency movements.

Involving finance leadership directly in setting hedging thresholds, rather than delegating the decision entirely to treasury staff, keeps the policy aligned with the organisation's actual risk tolerance.

Documenting the rationale behind each hedging decision creates an institutional record useful when reviewing the programme's effectiveness over subsequent years.
