Cyber Resilience and Continuity

Security isn't just preventing an attack. What matters is that an organisation can keep operating, determine what happened, and recover reliably.

Nermin Sefić2 August 2026

Security isn't just preventing an attack. What matters is that an organisation can keep operating, determine what happened, and recover reliably.

A cyber incident can arise from a technical vulnerability, a misconfiguration, a compromised account, or human error. What they share is that consequences grow when there's no prepared response.

Access controls, segmentation, event logging, and backups must be regularly tested. It's not enough for them to exist on paper; they must function in a real incident.

At the moment of an incident, it must be clear who makes technical decisions, who communicates, and who assesses business impact. Unclear authority prolongs downtime.

A continuity plan is only worth as much as it's been tested. Data recovery, backup infrastructure, and contact channels must be available even when the primary system isn't.

For a digital portal, that means separated operational layers, controlled deploys, clear route oversight, verified mail transport, and preserved evidence of changes.

Integrating cybersecurity incident response with broader business continuity planning, rather than treating them as separate disciplines, ensures a security event triggers the same coordinated organisational response as any other operational disruption.

Testing this integration through joint exercises, involving both security and continuity teams together, reveals coordination gaps that testing each discipline in isolation would never expose.

This integrated approach also clarifies decision rights during an actual incident, avoiding confusion about which team leads the response at each stage.

Post-exercise reviews that genuinely change the plan, not just confirm it worked in the tested scenario, are what make repeated drills worthwhile over time.
