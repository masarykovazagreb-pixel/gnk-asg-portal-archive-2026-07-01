The Risk of Dependence on a Single Technology Vendor

Dependence on a single technology vendor rarely results from one major decision — it builds gradually, and its true cost becomes visible only when an organisation wants to leave.

Nermin SefićAugust 8, 2026
When a company decides to replace a key business system — an ERP platform, a CRM, or the financial software daily operations run on — the cost of the licence and implementation itself rarely represents the largest share of the total price. Dependence on a single technology vendor, known as vendor lock-in, can create a cost invisible in the initial budget, one that only materialises the moment the organisation wants to change vendors or adapt the system to new needs.

How dependence on a single vendor forms

Dependence rarely results from one major decision — instead, it builds gradually, through hundreds of smaller decisions that each look reasonable on their own. Customising a system to specific company needs, integrating it with other internal tools, training staff on a specific interface — each of these steps raises the cost of future migration, and none individually looks significant enough to justify refusing useful functionality for the sake of preserving flexibility.Technology vendors consciously structure their products in ways that deepen this dependence — proprietary data formats that make export harder, deep integration with other products from the same vendor, or business models that reward long-term contracts with significantly better pricing than short-term ones. None of this is necessarily malicious, but the cumulative effect is dependence that becomes more expensive to unwind over time.

Hidden migration costs a budget rarely anticipates

When an organisation finally decides to change vendors, the migration cost includes far more than simply procuring the new system. Exporting and cleaning existing data, often accumulated over many years in a format specific to the old system, takes time and expertise that rarely exists internally. Rebuilding integrations with other systems, which accumulated around the old vendor over time, adds another layer of complexity rarely fully anticipated in an initial migration cost estimate.Training staff on the new system, alongside a temporary productivity dip while teams adapt to a new interface and processes, represents a cost rarely formally recorded but that genuinely affects business results during the transition period. Organisations that fail to include this cost in migration viability analysis systematically underestimate the total price of switching vendors, making them more inclined to stay with an existing solution even when a change would be more rational long-term.

Assessing dependency risk before signing a contract

The best time to assess vendor-dependency risk is before signing a contract, not after several years of use when the dependency is already deeply embedded in operational processes. Questions worth asking upfront include: in what format is data stored and how simple is its export, do standard APIs exist that enable integration with alternative systems, and what is the vendor's history regarding price changes once clients are already deeply integrated into their ecosystem.GNK ASG d.o.o. explicitly weighs exit-ability as an equal criterion alongside functionality and price when assessing new technology investments within the GNK DINAMO Ltd. Group, recognising that a solution that looks ideal today can become an operational burden if leaving it, whenever that becomes necessary, carries a disproportionately high cost.

Contractual provisions that reduce dependency risk

Technology vendor contracts can include specific provisions that pre-emptively reduce future dependency risk — an obligation for the vendor to enable data export in a standard, open format, a clearly defined price and timeline for assisted migration if the client decides to leave, or a cap on annual price increases instead of unlimited vendor discretion.Negotiating these provisions requires a negotiating position that is strongest before signing the first contract, when the vendor does not yet have a client already integrated and is therefore less inclined to walk away over additional requirements. Organisations that defer these questions until they are already deeply integrated into the system find their negotiating position for such changes considerably weaker than it was at the start of the relationship.

Open standards as a strategic advantage

Systems built on open, industry-accepted standards rather than proprietary, closed formats offer natural protection against deep dependence on a single vendor. Even when an organisation chooses a particular vendor's commercial solution, checking whether that solution uses open standards for data storage and exchange significantly affects future flexibility, regardless of whether that flexibility is ever actually exercised.This advantage is not always obvious at the point of system selection, when functionality and price are usually the dominant decision criteria, but it becomes decisive years later, when business needs change in ways the original system can no longer satisfy, and the organisation faces the question of how much a switch to an alternative would actually cost.

Diversifying critical systems across the technology portfolio

Organisations with multiple critical business systems can reduce overall dependency risk by diversifying vendors across the portfolio, rather than concentrating all key functions with a single vendor for the sake of supposedly simpler integration. While integration within a single vendor's ecosystem can indeed be technically simpler, that simplicity comes at the price of complete dependence on that one vendor's business and technical stability for the entirety of critical operations.The balance between simplicity of integration within a single ecosystem and the risk of concentrated dependence on one vendor has no universally correct answer — it depends on organisational size, the criticality of individual systems, and the organisation's own risk assessment. What is universally useful is that this decision be made consciously, after explicit assessment of the trade-offs, not as an accidental outcome of a series of uncoordinated individual procurement decisions.

Regularly assessing existing risk exposure

Organisations that already carry significant dependence on particular technology vendors can still reduce future risk through regular assessment of that exposure — which systems would be hardest to replace, what migration would cost right now, and whether there are steps that can be taken now to make a future migration, if ever needed, cheaper and less risky.This assessment need not immediately result in migration toward alternative systems — it is often enough for the organisation to simply better understand its own risk exposure, so that information can feed into future decisions about expanding use of an existing vendor or seeking an alternative for new needs that arise.

The role of internal documentation in reducing risk

Thoroughly documenting how data is structured within each critical system, what customisations have been made and why, and what integrations have been built with other systems, significantly reduces the cost of future migration regardless of whether that migration ever actually happens. This documentation, while requiring time to create and maintain, represents an investment that pays off precisely at the moment it becomes needed, a moment notoriously difficult to predict in advance.Organisations that build this documentation continuously, as part of regular operational practice, rather than trying to reconstruct it only once migration actually appears on the horizon, enter vendor-change negotiations with a considerably stronger position, because they have a clear picture of their own needs instead of depending entirely on information provided by the vendor itself, whose interest lies in making that migration look as complex and expensive as possible.

Conclusion — balancing convenience and flexibility

Completely avoiding dependence on technology vendors is neither a realistic nor a desirable goal — deep integration with a reliable vendor brings real operational advantages that should not be sacrificed for an abstract principle of flexibility. The goal is not to eliminate dependence, but to manage it consciously — understanding the true price of that dependence, building in protective mechanisms where reasonably possible, and regularly assessing whether the balance between convenience and risk remains acceptable as the business and the technology market change over time.

Vendor lock-in in the context of cloud infrastructure

Moving operations onto cloud infrastructure, while bringing significant operational advantages, introduces a specific form of dependence that differs from traditional software. Applications developed using a particular provider's proprietary services — specific databases, orchestration systems, or serverless functions with no direct equivalent among competing providers — become progressively harder to migrate as the number of these specific dependencies grows during product development.The decision to use a particular cloud provider's proprietary services instead of generic, portable alternatives often brings genuine advantages in development speed and operational simplicity, but that advantage must be consciously weighed against future flexibility. Organisations that skip this conscious assessment often find themselves, after several years of growth, in a situation where migrating to another cloud provider would require nearly complete redesign of the application, not just a technical adjustment.

Multi-cloud strategy as a response to risk concentration

Some organisations respond to single-cloud-provider risk by adopting a multi-cloud strategy — deliberately distributing workloads across multiple providers to avoid complete dependence on any single one. This strategy provides real protection against risk, but also considerable additional complexity in management, security monitoring, and staff training, since teams must know the specifics of multiple different platforms instead of just one.The decision to adopt a multi-cloud approach must weigh the actual likelihood of a scenario that would justify that added complexity — for most organisations, the risk of complete loss of access to a single cloud provider is low enough that the additional operational cost of a multi-cloud approach does not justify the benefit, while for organisations with exceptionally high business-continuity requirements, that same calculation can yield the opposite result.

Contractual protection specific to cloud services

Cloud infrastructure contracts should include clear provisions for a notice period before significant price changes or the discontinuation of support for a given service, as well as a guaranteed window during which the client can export its data after the relationship with the provider ends. Without these provisions, an organisation remains exposed to the risk that a provider unilaterally changes terms in a way that significantly raises cost or reduces functionality, with little time to react before the change takes effect.GNK ASG d.o.o. insists on a minimum ninety-day notice period before any significant change to cloud infrastructure terms that could affect business continuity, within its own contracts across the GNK DINAMO Ltd. Group, recognising that shorter periods leave too little time for an informed response, whether through negotiation or preparation for migration to an alternative.

Dependence on specific staff as a hidden form of lock-in

Vendor lock-in does not refer only to technical dependence on a system, but also to organisational dependence on specific employees who are the only ones on the team who truly understand how a given system works. This form of dependence, though less frequently discussed in the context of technology decisions, can be just as risky as technical dependence on a vendor — an organisation that loses a key employee with deep knowledge of a specific, customised system faces real operational risk regardless of whether the system itself is technically flexible.Systematically documenting knowledge and regularly rotating responsibility within the team, so more than one person understands critical systems, reduces this form of risk without requiring a change to the technology solution itself. This practice requires deliberate effort, since teams naturally tend toward specialisation developing organically, with individual employees gradually becoming the sole experts on a given system simply because they have worked with it the most.

Assessing market maturity before accepting dependence

The risk of dependence on a single vendor varies with the maturity of the market for a given type of technology — in mature, competitive markets with multiple comparable alternatives, dependence on a single vendor carries lower risk because migration to a competitor, while still requiring effort, remains a realistically viable option. In newer, less mature markets dominated by a small number of vendors or even just one, the same level of dependence carries considerably greater risk because genuine alternatives may not even exist at the moment they would be needed.This market-maturity assessment should be part of the formal analysis before adopting new technology, especially for critical business systems — an organisation that adopts technology early from an emerging, immature market accepts higher dependency risk in exchange for the potential competitive advantage of early adoption, a trade-off that should be a conscious, explicit decision, not an accidental outcome of enthusiasm about new technology.

An internal platform as an alternative to external dependence

For certain critical functions, organisations with sufficient internal technical capacity may consider developing their own, internal solution instead of relying on an external vendor, eliminating dependence on that vendor at the price of taking on full responsibility for developing and maintaining the system. This decision carries its own set of trade-offs — an internally developed system eliminates dependence on an external vendor, but creates dependence on the internal team that built it and must continuously maintain it.The choice between internal development and external procurement rarely has an unambiguously correct answer, but the analysis should include not just an immediate cost comparison, but a long-term assessment of dependency risk in both scenarios — external dependence on a vendor whose business decisions the organisation does not control, versus internal dependence on retaining key staff and continuously investing in maintenance of a system a vendor would otherwise update as part of their regular offering.

Continuously monitoring market alternatives

Even when an organisation decides to continue with its existing vendor, regularly monitoring the development of market alternatives maintains awareness of just how hard it would actually be to change course if that ever proved necessary, and provides an early signal if the market develops new options that could meaningfully change that calculation. Organisations that stop tracking alternatives entirely after making an initial vendor decision lose that awareness at precisely the moment it would be most useful — when a reason for considering a change arises.This continuous awareness need not involve actively testing alternatives or spending resources on parallel evaluation — a regular, perhaps annual, brief review of the market landscape is enough to maintain an informed picture of what options would be available if the current vendor relationship ever needed to be reconsidered, rather than starting that assessment from scratch only once a problem with the existing vendor has already emerged.

Switching cost as a measure of dependency depth

A useful practical indicator of dependency depth is estimating how much time and resources would be required to adapt a key business process if the current system stopped being available tomorrow. Organisations that cannot clearly answer that question, because they have never formally conducted that assessment, are usually more deeply dependent than they think — the very inability to quickly estimate an answer is often itself a sign that dependence has become so deeply embedded in daily work that it has become invisible.Conducting this assessment, even as a theoretical exercise without any intention of actual migration, often reveals assumptions and dependencies the team uses daily but has never explicitly articulated before — a discovery that has value in itself, regardless of whether it ever leads to an actual vendor change.

The board's role in overseeing strategic dependence

Decisions to adopt new critical technology systems are too often made at the operational level, without formally involving the board in assessing the strategic dependency risk that decision creates for the organisation as a whole. A technical team choosing a system based on functionality and ease of implementation does not necessarily have the broader context on the strategic importance of flexibility for the organisation's future, context that should factor into decisions about systems that will become foundational to the business for years to come.GNK ASG d.o.o. requires that any new technology investment above a defined threshold of value or strategic importance undergo a formal board-level dependency-risk assessment, not just a technical functionality evaluation, ensuring the long-term consequences of the decision are explicitly considered before the organisation takes on a commitment that will shape its technological flexibility for years ahead.

Concluding thought on the decision's time horizon

Technology decisions that today look like purely operational matters often carry strategic consequences that extend far beyond the usual annual planning horizon — a system chosen this year for short-term convenience can shape the organisation's operational flexibility five or ten years from now, long after the circumstances that originally justified that choice have ceased to be relevant. Weighing that longer time horizon at the moment the decision is made, not only after the dependence has already proven problematic, remains the most reliable way to keep dependency risk under reasonable control.

Communicating dependency risk to investors and partners

Transparency about strategic dependence on key technology vendors is increasingly expected by serious investors and business partners, especially for organisations whose operations depend significantly on digital infrastructure. An investor assessing the long-term viability of a business model reasonably wants to understand how exposed the organisation is if a key technology vendor changes terms, faces its own business troubles, or simply ceases to exist.

Organisations that proactively communicate their assessment and management of this risk, rather than waiting for an investor to raise it during due diligence, build an impression of mature, deliberate management that extends beyond a purely technological question and becomes part of a broader narrative about the quality of corporate governance across the organisation as a whole.

A practical framework for periodic dependency review

Organisations seeking to systematically manage dependency risk can introduce a simple, periodic review process — once a year, for each critical technology system, the team responsible answers three questions: how hard would migration to an alternative be if it ever became necessary, do genuinely comparable alternatives currently exist in the market, and has that dependence deepened or remained at a similar level over the past year. This simple but consistently applied process builds continuous awareness without demanding extensive resources.

Results of this periodic review should be aggregated at the organisational level, allowing the board to see the overall picture of strategic dependence across all critical systems, not just isolated assessments within each team. That aggregated picture reveals patterns that may not be visible at the level of any single system — for example, if it turns out that three different critical systems all depend on the same, single infrastructure provider, that concentration of risk only becomes visible once the individual assessments are considered together.

Organisations that apply this practice consistently, year after year, gradually develop an almost instinctive sense for recognising the moment when a decision that looks simple actually carries a significant hidden cost to future flexibility — a capability that is not acquired by reading guidance once, but through repeated practice of consciously asking the right questions before every new technology investment.
