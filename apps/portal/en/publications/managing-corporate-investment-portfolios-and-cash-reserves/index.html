Managing Corporate Investment Portfolios and Cash Reserves

Cash reserves that just sit in a current account lose value to inflation faster than it might first appear.

1 August 2026

Cash reserves that just sit in a current account lose value to inflation faster than it might first appear.

Companies with liquidity above their operational needs often simply leave the reserves in a current account, missing out on returns they could earn with minimal added liquidity risk.

Defining a clear liquidity hierarchy — funds needed immediately, funds needed within three months, funds that can be tied up for longer — enables structured management of surplus cash.

Conservative instruments such as government bonds or short-term time deposits provide a return while keeping high liquidity for the portion of reserves not needed in the short term.

The corporate reserves investment policy should be written down and approved at board level, not left to ad hoc decisions by an individual in the finance function.

Clear investment policy statements, reviewed periodically against changing risk tolerance, prevent ad hoc decisions from gradually drifting the portfolio away from its intended risk profile.

Regular rebalancing against a documented policy, rather than reactive adjustment to market conditions, tends to produce more consistent long-term outcomes.

This documented discipline also simplifies board oversight, since deviations from policy become immediately visible rather than requiring detailed investigation to detect.

Boards that receive regular reporting against this documented policy, not just periodic ad hoc updates, maintain clearer oversight of whether the organisation's actual practice matches its stated risk tolerance. This visibility becomes particularly valuable during market volatility, when the temptation to deviate from policy is strongest.

This oversight becomes indispensable during precisely the volatile periods when policy discipline matters most.
