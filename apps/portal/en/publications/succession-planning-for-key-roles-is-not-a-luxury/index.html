Succession Planning for Key Roles Is Not a Luxury

Organisations of every size are exposed to risk when knowledge and authority over key decisions remain concentrated in one person without a clear continuity plan.

Nermin Sefić3 August 2026

Concentrating key knowledge and decision-making authority in one person, without a documented plan for their extended absence, poses a risk regardless of organisational size.

A succession plan need not mean formally naming a successor in advance, but it must clearly define who takes on which authority and responsibility under different scenarios of temporary or permanent absence.

Documenting key business processes and relationships, rather than relying solely on an individual's personal memory, makes an organisation more resilient to changes in management composition.

The GNK DINAMO Ltd. group, with an authorized representative and UBO clearly defined for the parent company, builds a transparent governance structure through documentation available in the public registry.

Organisations without a documented succession plan for critical roles carry a form of operational risk that only becomes visible, often expensively, at the moment a key person actually leaves.

This risk applies equally to smaller organisations, where a single key person's departure can carry proportionally larger operational impact.

Smaller organisations, in particular, benefit from formalising this planning specifically because their leaner structures leave fewer natural backups for critical functions.

Organisations that treat succession planning as standard risk management, comparable to other forms of business continuity planning, avoid the scramble that unplanned departures otherwise create regardless of organisational size. This discipline, though requiring modest ongoing attention, provides protection against a risk that every organisation eventually faces in some form.

This protection matters regardless of organisational size or apparent operational stability.

This protection scales in importance precisely as organisational structures grow leaner and more specialised.
