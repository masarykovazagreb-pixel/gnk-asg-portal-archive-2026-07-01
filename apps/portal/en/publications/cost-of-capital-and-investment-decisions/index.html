Cost of Capital and Investment Decisions

A project that looks profitable by nominal return can destroy value if it doesn't exceed the company's weighted cost of capital.

Nermin Sefić2 August 2026

A project that looks profitable by nominal return can destroy value if it doesn't exceed the company's weighted cost of capital.

Weighted average cost of capital (WACC) represents the minimum return a project must achieve to justify the investment, accounting for both the cost of debt and the expected return required by equity holders. A project with a positive nominal return but a return below WACC formally reduces company value, even when it looks profitable on paper.

Consistently applying a single discount rate across all capital projects, rather than adjusting the rate to fit a given project's ambitions, prevents the systematic favouring of projects that are politically desirable rather than economically justified. Regularly updating WACC as interest rates change further protects that discipline.

Applying a single, consistently updated discount rate across all capital projects prevents the systematic favouring of politically convenient initiatives over those that are genuinely economically justified.

Regularly updating the discount rate as interest rates shift protects this discipline further, since a rate calculated years earlier under different conditions can quietly misstate which projects actually create value today.

Consistency in this discipline, maintained across economic cycles, distinguishes organisations with genuine capital discipline from those that only apply it when convenient.

Organisations that publish their WACC methodology internally, making it visible to anyone proposing a capital project, tend to see fewer politically motivated proposals reach serious consideration in the first place.

This transparency also builds internal trust in the capital allocation process itself, since project sponsors can see the same standard applied consistently to every proposal.
