AI Act: Operational Readiness for Companies

AI-rule compliance starts with mapping actual model use, process owners, and the consequences of an error.

Nermin Sefić2 August 2026

AI-rule compliance starts with mapping actual model use, process owners, and the consequences of an error.

An organisation can't manage AI risk if it doesn't know where AI is actually used. An official list of procured tools isn't enough, since models also appear inside existing platforms, add-ons, and external services.

Operational readiness requires an owner for each use case, data classification, a description of human oversight, an incident-reporting procedure, and a rule for withdrawing an unreliable result.

Documentation must not be built only for the regulator. It must help an employee understand when they may use a model, what data they must not input, and who confirms the final decision.

The best compliance emerges when rules follow the actual workflow, not when they exist in a document separate from the process.

This publication is an original analysis; the sources cited serve as reference documentation.

Companies that begin classifying their AI systems by risk category well before enforcement deadlines avoid the scramble that typically follows regulatory announcements, when compliance teams are stretched thin across every affected department at once.

Documentation of the classification process itself, not just its conclusion, matters when a regulator later asks how a given system was assessed.

Cross-functional involvement, spanning legal, technical, and business teams, tends to produce more accurate classifications than any single department attempting the assessment alone.

Organisations that leave this classification work until close to enforcement deadlines routinely discover it takes considerably longer than anticipated once every affected system is properly catalogued.
