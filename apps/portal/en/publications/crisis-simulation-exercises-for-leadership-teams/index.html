Crisis Simulation Exercises for Leadership Teams

Leadership that has never gone through a genuine crisis simulation discovers its weaknesses only when the cost of that discovery is highest — during an actual crisis.

Nermin SefićAugust 12, 2026
Leadership that has never gone through a genuine crisis-simulation exercise discovers its real weaknesses only in the moment of an actual crisis, when the cost of that discovery is highest. Simulation exercises, conducted seriously and regularly, offer a chance to uncover and correct those weaknesses in a controlled environment, before a real event tests leadership under conditions that allow no room for error without real consequences.

Why leadership often avoids these exercises

A genuine crisis simulation requires leadership to accept a scenario in which they look unprepared, perhaps even incompetent, in front of their own colleagues — an admission many executives instinctively avoid, preferring the comfort of assuming that, when a real crisis arrives, they will know how to respond. This psychological barrier, though understandable, represents one of the biggest risks to an organisation's real readiness, since that assumed competence remains untested until it is tested under pressure.Organisations that successfully overcome this barrier usually do so by framing simulation exercises as a learning opportunity, not an exam to pass or fail — a reframing that changes how executives approach the exercise, making them more willing to admit real shortcomings instead of performing a confidence that does not exist.

The difference between a technical exercise and a leadership exercise

Technical teams often run disaster-recovery drills focused on technical systems — restoring data, restarting servers, testing backup infrastructure. Exercises designed for leadership require a different focus — decision-making under uncertainty, communicating with stakeholders when information is incomplete, and coordinating across different organisational functions that must collaborate without a clear, pre-prepared script telling everyone exactly what to do.This difference means leadership exercises cannot simply be borrowed from a technical disaster-recovery manual — they require deliberate design that places executives in situations of genuine decision-making, with limited information and time pressure that reflects the real nature of a crisis, not just a check of familiarity with a technical protocol.

The element of surprise as a key component

An exercise whose scenario leadership knows in advance, with clearly defined steps to follow, tests the ability to execute a known protocol, not the actual judgement under uncertainty that characterises real crises. The element of surprise — an unknown exercise date, an unknown scenario nature, information revealed gradually rather than all known in advance — is key to gaining an authentic insight into how leadership would genuinely react when facing the unforeseen.GNK ASG d.o.o. runs irregular, unannounced simulation exercises across the GNK DINAMO Ltd. Group precisely for this reason — an announced exercise, however well designed, loses a significant share of its diagnostic value because participants get a chance to prepare in advance in a way a real crisis never would allow.

The role of an external facilitator in exercise design

Exercises designed and led by someone outside the organisation, with no personal stake in the outcome or knowledge of internal politics that could subtly shape the scenario, usually provide a more objective, challenging assessment than internally designed exercises. An external facilitator can ask questions and pose scenarios that an internal designer might, consciously or not, avoid out of a desire to shield colleagues from too uncomfortable a test.Choosing an external facilitator with experience across different industries and types of crises brings added value — a perspective that recognises patterns and vulnerabilities an internal team, focused solely on its own industry and past crises, might not recognise as relevant to its own situation.

Simulating media and public communication

A genuine crisis-simulation exercise must include an outward-facing communication element — simulated journalists asking difficult questions, simulated demands for a public statement under time pressure, and an assessment of how leadership's answers would sound if they were actually published. This element of the exercise often reveals a gap between what leadership thinks it would say during a crisis and what it actually says when under pressure from simulated press questioning.Recording these simulated communication moments, followed by a review together with leadership, offers a concrete, tangible insight into communication habits that would otherwise go unnoticed until they appear before a real audience during an actual crisis.

Testing the limits of delegated authority

A crisis often requires rapid decisions that under normal circumstances would need approval from the highest level of management. A simulation exercise provides an opportunity to test whether the boundaries of delegated authority during a crisis are clearly defined, or whether the team must waste precious time seeking approval for decisions that should have been pre-delegated to whoever is first in contact with the developing crisis.Discovering that the boundaries of delegation are unclear, or too strictly defined for the speed a real crisis demands, is one of the most common and most valuable findings of simulation exercises — a finding considerably cheaper to fix before an actual crisis than to discover during one, when delayed decision-making directly increases the damage.

Psychological safety during and after the exercise

An exercise that exposes leadership to real pressure must simultaneously maintain the psychological safety that allows participants to admit mistakes and uncertainty without fear of long-term professional consequences. A culture in which mistakes made during an exercise are later used against participants in performance reviews destroys the value of future exercises, since participants become motivated to perform competence rather than honestly demonstrate the real limits of their knowledge and ability.A clear, pre-communicated policy that the exercise exists solely for learning, combined with consistent follow-through on that policy in actual post-exercise conduct, builds the trust that enables more authentic, more valuable participation in future exercises.

Conclusion — an exercise as an investment in a moment that cannot be predicted

The cost and time invested in a genuine crisis simulation is hard to justify in advance, since its real value only shows up in the moment of an actual crisis that, by definition, cannot be precisely predicted. Organisations that nonetheless invest in these exercises, consistently and seriously, build leadership that enters a real crisis with experience, not just theory, of what decision-making under pressure looks like — experience that cannot be gained any other way except through genuine, if simulated, practice.

Designing scenarios that genuinely test limits

A scenario that is too easy, one leadership resolves without serious effort, provides a false sense of readiness with no real diagnostic value. A scenario that is too hard, exceeding the realistic possibilities of any reasonable response, can demoralise participants without offering useful insight into how to improve actual readiness. The art of designing an effective exercise lies in finding a scenario demanding enough to reveal genuine weaknesses, yet realistic enough that the results have practical application to the organisation's actual context.This balancing act requires deep understanding of the organisation's specific vulnerabilities — a generic crisis scenario pulled from a manual, without adaptation to the organisation's actual industry, size, and operational context, rarely achieves that balance as well as a scenario carefully designed with full regard for the specifics of the particular organisation running it.

Involving multiple levels of the organisation in the exercise

Exercises that involve only the most senior level of management miss the opportunity to test coordination between that level and the operational teams who, during a real crisis, would need to execute the decisions management makes. A realistic exercise should include at least simulated interaction with that operational layer, even if the operational teams themselves do not directly participate in the full scope of the exercise, so that management gets a genuine sense of how quickly and precisely its decisions can be translated into actual action.This broader perspective reveals problems in the communication chain that an isolated exercise focused solely on management would never uncover — a decision that sounds clear when management makes it can become ambiguous or unworkable by the time it reaches the operational team responsible for executing it, a loss of clarity best revealed precisely through a realistic simulation of that information transfer.

The financial dimension often overlooked

Crisis-simulation exercises often focus on operational and communication aspects, neglecting the financial dimension that equally requires fast, under-pressure judgement — questions like the availability of emergency financial resources, authority for unplanned expenditure during a crisis, or communication with banks and investors about the financial consequences of a crisis situation. Leadership that has never simulated these questions may discover only during an actual crisis that the organisation's financial processes simply were not designed for the speed a crisis demands.GNK ASG d.o.o. includes the chief financial officer and relevant members of the finance team in every crisis-simulation exercise across the GNK DINAMO Ltd. Group, ensuring the financial dimension of the response is tested just as thoroughly as the operational and communication dimensions, rather than remaining an afterthought resolved only once a real crisis reveals its importance.

Post-exercise analysis as a critical, often neglected step

An exercise's value lies not just in running it, but in the thorough, honest analysis that follows — what worked, what didn't, and what concrete changes the organisation needs to make based on those findings. Organisations that run an exercise but skip this follow-up analysis, or conduct it superficially without a genuine intention to apply the lessons learned, lose most of the value the exercise could have offered.A structured post-exercise analysis should result in a concrete, time-bound action plan with clearly assigned owners for each identified weakness — a document actively tracked to completion, not just a report filed away and forgotten until the next exercise, or worse, an actual crisis, once again reveals the same, unresolved problems.

Exercise frequency and the risk of overload

Too-frequent crisis-simulation exercises can exhaust leadership and reduce participants' genuine engagement, who over time begin to experience the exercises as a routine formality rather than a serious learning opportunity. Too-infrequent exercises, on the other hand, leave gaps too long during which acquired knowledge and reflexes can fade, especially when leadership composition changes between exercises.Finding the right frequency requires a balance specific to each organisation — factors like the pace of internal change, the level of risk the organisation is exposed to, and the experience of current leadership all affect how often exercises need to run to retain their value without becoming an overwhelming routine participants approach without genuine attention.

Leadership's role in modelling behaviour during the exercise

How senior executives approach the exercise directly affects how seriously the entire organisation takes the crisis-simulation process. An executive who treats the exercise as an unnecessary nuisance, visibly disinterested or disengaged during its running, sends a clear message to the rest of the organisation that they themselves do not believe in the value of that process — a message that quickly spreads and undermines every other participant's engagement.Executives who, by contrast, actively model serious, engaged participation, including their own willingness to admit mistakes and uncertainty during the exercise, build a culture in which the entire organisation accepts simulation exercises as a valuable, legitimate part of preparing for real challenges, not as a formality to survive before returning to 'actual work'.

Adapting scenarios to specific geopolitical and market conditions

Scenarios that were relevant a few years ago can lose relevance as geopolitical relationships, market conditions, and the nature of industry-specific threats change. Regularly updating scenarios, with attention to the current state of the world in which the organisation actually operates, ensures exercises remain relevant to real risks, not to an outdated picture of risk that no longer reflects current reality.

This adaptation requires continuous monitoring of the broader context beyond the organisation itself — geopolitical tensions, regulatory change, emerging technological threats — to ensure the next exercise tests leadership against a scenario that genuinely reflects threats the organisation could realistically face, not one that was relevant in some past, now outdated context.

Involving the board or supervisory body in the process

An organisation's board or supervisory body rarely participates directly in operational crisis-simulation exercises, but understanding how management reacts under pressure, and the actual quality of the organisation's preparedness, represents valuable information for their oversight role. Regularly briefing the board on crisis-simulation results, including an honest presentation of discovered weaknesses, not just successes, builds a more transparent relationship between management and the supervisory body regarding the organisation's real state of resilience.

This transparency toward the board also creates an additional incentive for management to take exercises and follow-up corrective measures seriously, knowing results will be shared with a body that holds formal oversight responsibility for the organisation's risk management as a whole.

Concluding thoughts on the long-term value of the practice

Organisations that treat crisis simulation as a continuous practice, embedded in the regular rhythm of risk management, rather than an occasional, isolated activity, gradually build an organisational culture in which serious thinking about crises and preparedness is considered a normal, expected part of responsible management, not an exceptional, rare activity conducted only when external pressure demands it. This cultural shift, built through consistent practice over time, ultimately represents perhaps an even more valuable outcome of simulation exercises than any single lesson learned through a specific scenario.

Adaptation for rapidly expanding organisations

Organisations going through rapid growth face a particular challenge in maintaining the relevance of crisis-simulation exercises — leadership that participated in a past exercise may no longer be the same leadership that would respond to an actual crisis, since the organisation has meanwhile grown, added new functions, or changed its structure of responsibility. Regularly updating the list of participants, aligned with the organisation's actual current structure, prevents a situation where the exercise tests an outdated structure that no longer reflects reality.

This challenge requires that crisis-simulation planning be connected to the broader organisational planning process, not isolated from it — any significant change in leadership structure or responsibility should automatically trigger a review of whether the next planned exercise remains relevant to the organisation's new, changed reality.

When an exercise reveals uncomfortable truths about leadership itself

Sometimes a crisis-simulation exercise reveals that the problem is not solely in processes or protocols, but in the composition or dynamics of leadership itself — an individual who systematically takes on too much control under pressure, a personality conflict that worsens under stress, or a lack of trust between team members that hinders fast, coordinated action. These findings, while uncomfortable, represent some of the most valuable insights an exercise can offer, since they address risks that would otherwise remain hidden until a real crisis mercilessly exposes them.

Organisations willing to seriously address these harder, more personal findings, including a possible change to leadership composition or roles if the exercise clearly shows this is needed, extract full value from the simulation process. Those that ignore or downplay such findings, focusing solely on easier, procedural fixes, miss the chance to address precisely the risks that could have the greatest impact on the outcome of an actual crisis.

The role of written protocols arising from lessons learned

Every exercise that reveals a genuine shortcoming should result in a concrete revision of the organisation's written protocols, not just a verbal agreement that things will be done differently "next time". A written protocol, accessible to everyone who would need to use it during an actual crisis, ensures a learned lesson survives changes in leadership composition and does not depend solely on the memory of individuals who participated in the exercise that revealed the need for change.

Organisations that regularly update their crisis protocols based on exercise findings, rather than leaving protocols unchanged for years, build a document that genuinely reflects the organisation's accumulated experience and knowledge, not a static text written once and never seriously revisited in light of actual practice.

Measuring return on investment in simulation exercises

Justifying continued investment in crisis-simulation exercises to management or the board requires some form of value measurement, even though direct return on investment is notoriously hard to quantify for an activity whose purpose is to prevent or mitigate future, uncertain events. Indirect indicators — improvement in response time across successive exercises, a reduction in identified weaknesses over time, or improved leadership confidence measured through structured questionnaires after each exercise — can provide a useful, if imperfect, picture of progress.

These measurements, tracked continuously over several years of practice, build a more compelling case for continued investment than a single, isolated assessment of one exercise, since they show a trend of improvement that is harder to dispute than a subjective impression of value based on one isolated experience.

A closing thought on the relationship between preparation and real resilience

No amount of simulation can perfectly predict or replicate an actual crisis, which almost always brings elements no pre-designed scenario could have fully anticipated. The value of simulation exercises lies not in perfectly predicting the future, but in building the capacity for adaptation, fast thinking under pressure, and coordinated action that remains relevant regardless of the specific nature of the crisis that actually occurs. That broader, transferable capability, built through repeated, serious practice, remains the most valuable outcome of any seriously conducted crisis-simulation exercise.

Organisations that consistently embrace this practice over years of repeated, serious effort are usually also the ones that, when a real crisis finally arrives, respond with a composure that looks almost natural — composure built not by chance, but through deliberate, patient investment in a readiness that never pretended to have skipped the process of genuine testing.

That composure, visible only in the moment it is genuinely tested, represents the ultimate, tangible proof that the time invested in a seemingly abstract, hard-to-measure practice of crisis simulation was time well spent, regardless of how difficult it may have been to prove its value in advance to anyone who has never actually gone through the process.

Regularly, honestly asking how well our team would actually perform under real pressure is therefore worth doing not as a one-off exercise, but as a lasting habit that, over time, becomes part of the organisation's very culture, embedded as deeply as any other foundational operational practice.

Organisations that build this habit tend to be the ones that, when the moment finally arrives, meet it with a quiet, well-earned readiness — not luck, but the accumulated result of choosing, year after year, to test themselves before circumstances force the test upon them.
