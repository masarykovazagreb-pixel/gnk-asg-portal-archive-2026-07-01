Cross-Border Taxation: Transfer Pricing

Transfer prices between related companies must reflect market conditions, not the group's internal priorities for allocating profit.

Nermin Sefić2 August 2026

Transfer prices between related companies must reflect market conditions, not the group's internal priorities for allocating profit.

When companies within the same group operate across multiple jurisdictions, the price one company charges another for a service or good must reflect terms two unrelated parties would agree — the so-called arm's-length principle. Deviating from that principle, even unintentionally, opens the question of income reclassification by tax authorities in multiple countries simultaneously.

Documenting transfer-pricing methodology before the transaction, rather than afterward as a defence during an inspection, reduces exposure to double taxation and penalties. Regularly reviewing that documentation in line with changes to the group's business model is just as important as setting it up initially.

Preparing transfer pricing documentation contemporaneously, at the time transactions occur rather than reconstructed later during an audit, produces a far more convincing record of arm's-length pricing than any retrospective justification could.

Tax authorities in multiple jurisdictions increasingly share information with each other, meaning inconsistent positions taken in different countries surface more readily than organisations might assume, making a coordinated, defensible global methodology essential rather than optional.

GNK ASG d.o.o. maintains this documentation as a continuous practice across the GNK DINAMO Ltd. Group, rather than compiling it retroactively when a jurisdiction requests it.

Local tax advisors familiar with each jurisdiction's specific enforcement practice remain essential, since statutory text alone rarely captures how rules are actually applied in practice.

Periodic internal review of the documentation itself, checked against evolving business operations, catches drift between what the paperwork describes and how the organisation actually functions today.
