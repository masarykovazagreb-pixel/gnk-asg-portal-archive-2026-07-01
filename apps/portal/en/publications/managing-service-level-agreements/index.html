Managing Service Level Agreements (SLAs)

An SLA never tested in practice offers a false sense of security until a real outage reveals the gap between promise and reality.

1 August 2026

An SLA never tested in practice offers a false sense of security until a real outage reveals the gap between promise and reality.

A service level agreement promising 99.9% uptime sounds convincing, but rarely contains a clear definition of how that uptime is measured and who verifies the supplier's reports.

Penalties for SLA breaches are usually capped at that month's service fee, often disproportionately small compared to the actual business damage from a service outage.

Independent monitoring of critical service availability, rather than relying solely on the supplier's own reports, reveals the real state of affairs and strengthens negotiating position at contract renewal.

A business continuity plan that doesn't assume perfect supplier availability, but includes a concrete response to a supplier outage, is more effective than relying solely on contract terms.

Regular review of actual performance against SLA commitments, not just contractual language, reveals whether agreements are being genuinely honoured or merely formally satisfied.

Involving the teams responsible for actual service delivery in SLA negotiation produces more realistic, more consistently honoured commitments than agreements negotiated by sales alone.

This collaborative negotiation approach also builds internal buy-in for meeting commitments, since delivery teams helped shape the standards they are now accountable to.

Organisations that revisit SLA terms periodically, not only at contract renewal, adapt more effectively to changing operational realities than those locked into commitments negotiated under circumstances that may no longer apply. This ongoing dialogue between commercial and delivery teams also prevents the gradual drift between contractual promise and operational capability.
