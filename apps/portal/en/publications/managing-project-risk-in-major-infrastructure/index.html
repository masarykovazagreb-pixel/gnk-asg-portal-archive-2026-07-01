Managing Project Risk in Major Infrastructure

A major infrastructure project without a formal risk register usually reveals problems only once they become expensive to fix.

Nermin Sefić1 August 2026

A major infrastructure project without a formal risk register usually reveals problems only once they become expensive to fix.

High-value infrastructure projects carry risks that materialise months or years after initial planning, often after the key people who originally made the decision have changed.

A formal risk register, updated at regular project steering committee meetings, ensures identified risks are actively tracked, not just written down once and forgotten.

A contingency reserve calculated from an actual risk assessment, not a flat percentage, more realistically reflects the likelihood and scale of possible deviations from plan.

An independent review of project progress at key milestones, outside the direct reporting line of the project team, uncovers problems that internal reports can systematically understate.

Independent risk review, conducted by people outside the immediate project team, surfaces concerns that those closest to a project may be reluctant to raise themselves.

This independent perspective proves most valuable early in a project, when course corrections remain considerably cheaper than they become later.

Structuring this review as a genuine challenge function, with real authority to flag concerns, distinguishes effective independent review from a purely ceremonial exercise.

This independent risk function works most effectively when granted genuine authority to escalate concerns directly to senior decision-makers, rather than filtering findings exclusively through the project team being reviewed. Organisations that preserve this independence see measurably better early detection of the risks most likely to derail major infrastructure commitments.

Genuine independence, not just formal process, is what makes this review function effectively.
