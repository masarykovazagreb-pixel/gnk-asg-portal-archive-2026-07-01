Currency Risk in International Business

Revenue, costs, and financing in different currencies can change a project's economics even without any change in operating performance.

Nermin Sefić2 August 2026

Revenue, costs, and financing in different currencies can change a project's economics even without any change in operating performance.

A company can meet its operating plan and still lose margin due to an exchange-rate change. Currency exposure arises at the moment of contracting, not only when an invoice is paid.

Risk management begins with mapping revenue, costs, debt, and capital investment by currency and by deadline.

Protection doesn't mean eliminating every change, but limiting the consequences to a level the project and the group can bear.

Contract clauses, natural flow matching, and financial instruments must all be part of a single plan.

Currency exposure should be reassessed regularly as the mix of revenues and costs across currencies shifts, not evaluated once and treated as a fixed, permanently valid conclusion.

Natural hedges, created by matching revenue and cost currencies where operationally possible, often reduce exposure more efficiently than financial instruments alone, though few organisations systematically pursue this option before reaching for derivatives.

Organisations that model several exchange rate scenarios before finalising major international commitments make more informed decisions than those relying on a single, static forecast.

This scenario planning becomes particularly valuable ahead of major commitments, such as long-term supply contracts denominated in a foreign currency.

Treasury teams that revisit exposure calculations quarterly, rather than annually, catch shifts in currency mix before they compound into larger unmanaged risk.

This discipline matters most during periods of heightened currency volatility, when the cost of delayed reassessment grows fastest.
