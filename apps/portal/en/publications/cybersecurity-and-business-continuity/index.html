Cybersecurity and Business Continuity

Securing a business system requires separated access, backups, logs, and a tested recovery plan.

Nermin Sefić2 August 2026

Securing a business system requires separated access, backups, logs, and a tested recovery plan.

Security is a business function, not just a technical matter. Within the GNK ASG d.o.o. and GNK DINAMO Ltd. system, it must be linked to clear accountability, demonstrable controls, and a documented recovery plan.

Practical implementation includes least-privilege access, multi-factor authentication, separated secrets, change logging, and a tested rollback. Every system must have a process owner, a backup, and a review deadline.

The greatest risk arises when there's a single administrator account, an unverified backup, or a hidden dependency on one individual. Automation and AI can prepare data and alerts, but must not independently make sensitive security decisions.

Resilience isn't proven by a document but by a verified ability to recover. This publication is part of the GNK ASG Intelligence Desk system and is informational in nature.

A continuity plan that addresses natural disasters and infrastructure failure but omits deliberate cyberattack as a distinct scenario leaves a meaningful gap in an increasingly relevant threat category.

Recovery time objectives calibrated for physical disruptions do not necessarily transfer directly to cyber incidents, which can require forensic investigation before recovery can safely begin at all.

Coordinating these two functions under shared leadership, rather than separate reporting lines, reduces the risk of gaps emerging between them during an actual crisis.

Clear escalation paths, defined and rehearsed in advance, reduce the confusion that otherwise slows response during the first critical hours of an incident.

Documenting lessons from each exercise, and tracking whether identified gaps are actually closed before the next drill, prevents the same weaknesses from resurfacing exercise after exercise.
