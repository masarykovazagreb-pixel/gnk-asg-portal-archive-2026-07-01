Valuing Intangible Assets in Acquisitions

In an acquisition, intangible assets like brand and customer base often make up a larger share of the price than tangible assets.

Nermin Sefić1 August 2026

In an acquisition, intangible assets like brand and customer base often make up a larger share of the price than tangible assets, yet are harder to value objectively.

In modern acquisitions, especially in tech and services, intangible assets — brand, customer relationships, proprietary technology — often exceed the value of tangible assets on the balance sheet.

Intangible-asset valuation methods (cost, market, income) yield different results, so transparency about the chosen method is key to both sides' trust in negotiations.

Overvaluing intangible assets to justify a higher purchase price creates a risk of subsequent impairment on the buyer's balance sheet, with a direct impact on future financial statements.

An independent valuation of intangible assets, conducted before final price negotiations, reduces room for later dispute over whether the price paid was justified.

Rigorous, independent valuation of intangible assets, rather than relying solely on the seller's characterisation, protects against overpaying for value that may not fully materialise.

This rigour matters most in transactions where intangible assets represent a disproportionate share of the total purchase price being considered.

Engaging valuation specialists with genuine sector expertise, not generalist appraisers, produces assessments considerably more likely to withstand later scrutiny.

Organisations that build genuine internal capability for intangible asset valuation, rather than relying entirely on external specialists for every transaction, develop institutional judgement that improves the quality of their own due diligence over successive acquisitions. This accumulated expertise proves particularly valuable in sectors where intangible assets consistently represent a disproportionate share of total transaction value.
