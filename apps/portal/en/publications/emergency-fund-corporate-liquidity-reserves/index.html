Emergency Fund: Corporate Liquidity Reserves

A company without a defined liquidity reserve handles every crisis through improvisation instead of a plan prepared in advance.

Nermin Sefić2 August 2026

A company without a defined liquidity reserve handles every crisis through improvisation instead of a plan prepared in advance.

A liquidity reserve defined as the number of months of operating costs a company can cover without new revenue provides a clear, measurable framework for financial resilience.

Without a predefined reserve, every unexpected crisis — a revenue drop, a delayed major payment, a sudden expense — is resolved through ad hoc borrowing on worse terms than would be available in advance.

The size of the required reserve depends on revenue volatility and business seasonality; companies with more stable revenue can safely maintain a smaller reserve than those exposed to cyclical shocks.

Regularly testing reserve adequacy through simulated revenue-drop scenarios turns liquidity planning from a one-time decision into an ongoing discipline.

Corporate liquidity reserves serve a function comparable to personal emergency funds, providing a buffer against disruption without requiring hasty, unfavourable financing decisions.

Sizing this reserve against realistic disruption scenarios, rather than an arbitrary percentage of revenue, produces a more genuinely useful buffer than generic industry benchmarks alone.

Reserves held in genuinely liquid instruments, not merely assets labelled as such, are what actually provide protection when a disruption arrives without warning.

Reviewing reserve adequacy at least annually, against updated disruption scenarios, keeps this buffer aligned with the organisation's actual current risk profile rather than assumptions made years earlier.

Clear governance around when and how reserves may be accessed prevents the fund from being gradually depleted for purposes other than genuine emergencies.
