Backup as the Last Line of Defense

A backup that has never been tested by actually restoring data offers a false sense of security, not real protection.

Nermin Sefić1 August 2026

A backup that has never been tested by actually restoring data offers a false sense of security, not real protection.

Regularly creating backups without periodically testing the actual data-restoration process leaves a company exposed to the risk that the backup simply won't work as expected at the moment of crisis.

Ransomware attacks increasingly target the backups themselves, making an isolated, offline, or immutable copy the last genuine line of defence once every other system is compromised.

The time required for a full system recovery from backup should be known and tested in advance — discovering that recovery takes days instead of hours during a real incident is a costly lesson.

A clearly defined policy on how long backups are retained and how often restoration is tested turns a backup from a formality into genuinely functional protection.

A backup that has never been tested through an actual restoration exercise offers only a false sense of security, since failures in the backup process itself often go undetected until the moment recovery is genuinely needed.

Scheduling restoration drills on the same cadence as other critical business continuity exercises, rather than treating them as a separate, lower-priority task, keeps this discipline from quietly lapsing over time.

A tested backup process is the only kind that can be trusted when it actually matters.

The frequency of restoration testing should scale with how critical the underlying data is, not default to a single organisation-wide schedule regardless of actual business impact.

Extending this testing discipline to third-party data held on an organisation's behalf closes a gap that purely internal backup audits often miss.
