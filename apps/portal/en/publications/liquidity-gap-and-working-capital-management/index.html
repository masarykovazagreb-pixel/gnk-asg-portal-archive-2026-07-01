The gap between the moment an invoice is issued and the moment it's collected determines how much external financing a company genuinely needs.

The cash conversion cycle — the time from paying a supplier to collecting from a customer — shows how long a company must finance its operations with its own or borrowed money before cash actually arrives. When collection days lengthen faster than supplier payment days, the gap grows and liquidity pressure becomes structural, not seasonal.

Shortening the gap isn't solved by more aggressive collection alone, but by aligning supplier payment terms, inventory dynamics, and customer credit-approval policy as a single system. Companies that track those three elements together, rather than separately by department, resort less often to short-term borrowing at moments that hurt their margin most.

Modelling working capital needs under stressed scenarios, not just normal operating conditions, reveals gaps that routine forecasting under stable assumptions would miss.

Regular scenario testing against a range of demand and payment timing assumptions reveals working capital vulnerabilities that single-scenario planning would miss.

Involving operational teams directly in this modelling, not just finance alone, produces more realistic assumptions grounded in actual business patterns.

This stress-tested approach to working capital planning proves particularly valuable ahead of major business decisions, such as significant capital expenditure or expansion into new markets. Organisations that model these scenarios in advance make more informed commitments than those relying solely on current, potentially optimistic, cash flow assumptions.

This ongoing discipline pays dividends precisely when conditions turn genuinely stressed and options narrow.
