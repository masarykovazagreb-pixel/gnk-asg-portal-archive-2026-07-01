Risk Culture and Board Tolerance

Home›Publications›Risk Culture and Board Tolerance

A risk-management policy without a clearly defined tolerance threshold leaves every decision to the judgment of whoever makes it.

2 August 2026

A risk-management policy without a clearly defined tolerance threshold leaves every decision to the judgment of whoever makes it.

A declarative policy of "low risk tolerance" without concrete, measurable thresholds per risk category gives employees no real guidance for day-to-day decisions. The result is inconsistency — one manager rejects a deal another would accept, both operating within the same formal policy.

A board that explicitly defines quantified thresholds — maximum allowed loss per transaction, maximum exposure to a single partner, the threshold for escalating a decision to a higher level — builds a risk culture that is predictable and independent of who happens to be making the decision that day.

Translating abstract risk tolerance statements into specific, quantified thresholds gives day-to-day decision-makers the concrete guidance a general policy statement alone cannot provide.

Regular review of these thresholds against actual outcomes helps the board calibrate whether stated risk tolerance matches genuine organisational appetite in practice.

This calibration exercise works best as a genuine, honest conversation, rather than a formality conducted to satisfy governance documentation requirements.

Organisations that treat this calibration as an ongoing dialogue between board and management, rather than a static policy document, maintain risk tolerance thresholds that genuinely reflect current organisational circumstances and strategic priorities. This living approach to risk governance proves considerably more useful in practice than thresholds set once and never meaningfully revisited.

Genuine dialogue, not documentation alone, is what makes this calibration meaningful in practice.

This ongoing calibration, revisited regularly, keeps stated tolerance genuinely aligned with organisational reality.
