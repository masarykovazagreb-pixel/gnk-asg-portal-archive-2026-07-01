Managing Open-Source Intellectual Property

An open-source license embedded in a commercial product without checking its terms can later restrict a company's rights over its own software.

Nermin Sefić1 August 2026

An open-source license embedded in a commercial product without checking its terms can later restrict a company's rights over its own software.

Using open-source libraries speeds up software development, but every license carries its own terms that can affect whether a company may keep exclusive rights over the final product.

Copyleft licenses like certain GPL versions can require that a company's own source code using them be released under the same license, often unacceptable for a commercial product.

A systematic inventory of all open-source components used and their licenses, updated with every dependency change, prevents an unpleasant surprise during due diligence or a company sale.

A clear internal policy on permitted license types, checked before introducing a new dependency into a project, is a cheaper preventive measure than later removing a problematic component.

Maintaining an accurate inventory of open source components in use, including their specific licence terms, prevents inadvertent violations that can carry significant legal and reputational consequences.

Automated scanning tools that flag license compliance issues during development, rather than after deployment, catch problems when they remain cheapest to address.

Building this awareness into developer training, not just relying on automated tools alone, creates a stronger organisational culture around license compliance.

This license awareness becomes increasingly important as organisations incorporate more third-party and open source components into their technology stack, each carrying its own specific compliance obligations. Building this awareness into standard development practice, rather than treating it as a separate legal review step, integrates compliance more naturally into daily engineering work.
