A Recovery Drill Only Works If It Surprises You

A cyber incident drill that goes perfectly according to script is more likely a sign of too lenient a scenario than real readiness.

Nermin Sefić3 August 2026

A test of a cyber incident response plan that goes perfectly according to script, without a single moment of confusion or unexpected obstacle, more often points to too lenient a test than to real organisational readiness.

The value of a drill lies precisely in uncovering weak points before a real incident, so a scenario that deliberately includes unforeseen elements gives a more reliable picture of a system's actual resilience.

This commentary is part of the GNK ASG Intelligence Desk system and is informational in nature.

A recovery drill that unfolds exactly as scripted tests the team's ability to perform a rehearsed routine, not its actual readiness for the unexpected — and unexpected is precisely what a real incident will be.

Building genuine unpredictability into a drill requires deliberate effort — someone has to design a scenario that even the exercise's organisers have not fully rehearsed — which is precisely why so many organisations default to the easier, scripted version instead. The extra effort pays off only when it matters least visibly: not in the drill itself, but in how the team performs during the real incident that the drill was meant to prepare them for.
