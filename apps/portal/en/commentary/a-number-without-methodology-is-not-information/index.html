A Number Without a Methodology Is Not Information

A financial indicator published without a clear explanation of how it was calculated is worth less than it appears at first glance.

Nermin Sefić30 July 2026

A financial indicator published without a clear explanation of how it was calculated is worth less than it appears at first glance.

Publishing a key indicator without an accompanying explanation of data sources and calculation method leaves room for differing interpretations and, ultimately, reduces the credibility of the figure itself, however accurate it may be.

Two companies can publish what looks like the same revenue figure and arrive at very different numbers depending on whether one-off items are included, how currency differences are treated, or on which date the reporting period closes — without that context, the figures are not comparable, even when both are accurate.

A disclosure standard must therefore include at minimum the data source, the period it covers, and the basic calculation method; only then can a reader assess reliability rather than guess at what sits behind an isolated figure.

When every key indicator is accompanied by a short explanation of its data source and calculation method, the reader can judge the information's reliability instead of taking it on faith.

This commentary is part of the GNK ASG Intelligence Desk system and is informational in nature.

The practical consequence for readers is that they should treat any headline figure — revenue growth, profit margin, market share — as provisional until the underlying methodology is available, rather than assuming good faith by default. For organisations on the disclosing side, the lesson is the mirror image: methodology should accompany the figure from the first publication, not be produced defensively after someone asks a difficult question about how the number was reached.
