A Sustainability Report Should Admit Its Shortcomings

Home›Commentary›A Sustainability Report Should Admit Its Shortcomings

A report listing only positive results, without a single area for improvement, loses credibility with a careful reader.

Nermin Sefić3 August 2026

A sustainability report that lists only positive results, without a single mention of an area requiring further improvement, usually raises doubt about the completeness of the picture in a careful reader, rather than trust.

Acknowledging concrete shortcomings, alongside a clear plan to address them, typically comes across as more credible than a report that appears as if the organisation has already achieved perfection in every measured area.

This commentary is part of the GNK ASG Intelligence Desk system and is informational in nature.

Acknowledging a shortfall, paired with a concrete plan for addressing it, builds more credibility than any run of favourable figures alone — because the reader then knows the report is a genuine account, not a marketing document.

Readers, investors, and regulators increasingly recognise the pattern of a report that lists only achievements, and that recognition itself has become a credibility risk — a suspiciously flawless report now invites more scrutiny, not less. An organisation that gets ahead of this by candidly naming where it still falls short, alongside a specific plan and timeline for improvement, converts a potential weakness into a demonstration of exactly the kind of governance maturity that sustainability reporting is meant to signal in the first place.
