AI Transparency and the Corporate Evidence Trail: What Businesses Should Prepare Before Autumn 2026

Regulatory dates may move, but the direction is clear: organisations need an inventory of AI tools, rules for public content, human review and a documented trail of important decisions.

GNK ASG AI assistant and digital operations · GNK ASG Visual Index

Artificial intelligence is no longer only a productivity tool. For companies operating in the European Union, it is increasingly a question of evidence. A business should be able to identify which service produced an output, what source material was used, what checks were performed and who accepted the final result.

The original EU AI Act timetable placed broad application of major obligations on 2 August 2026. A provisional agreement announced on 7 May 2026 proposed moving some dates, including the date for marking certain AI-generated outputs to 2 December 2026. Formal approval is still required. The practical conclusion is not to pause work, but to use the additional time to build a proportionate and documented system.

Transparency begins before publication

A visible notice on an image, video or text is only the last step. The earlier questions are more important. Does the company know which tools are being used? Are confidential materials protected? Are factual claims checked? Can the organisation identify the person who approved a public output? If those questions cannot be answered, a label alone does not create accountability.

The European Commission's General-Purpose AI Code of Practice is organised around transparency, copyright, safety and security. Participation in the Code is voluntary, while the relevant duties in the legislation are not. The value of the Code is practical: it offers organisations a structured way to prepare documentation and demonstrate a consistent approach.

Five practical controls

1. Maintain an AI inventory. Record public assistants, office-software features, image tools, translation services, analytics and internal agents. Each entry should have a business owner and a clear purpose.

2. Classify the use. A tool that improves wording is different from a system that materially shapes a customer, workforce or financial decision. Record the expected output, data categories, review level and potential consequence of an error.

3. Keep an evidence trail. For important use cases, retain the service or model version, date, key instructions, source material, checks and final approval. Not every informal interaction needs to be archived, but important public and business outputs need a reliable record.

4. Set publication rules. Decide when AI assistance is disclosed, when generated or altered material is marked, and when a specialist review is required. Apply the rule consistently to websites, social media, presentations, images, video and customer communication.

5. Review suppliers. Understand where information is processed, how long records are retained, whether inputs are reused, how changes are announced and what documentation is available to business customers.

Why the advertising debate matters

On 19 June 2026, Reuters reported that EuroCommerce had requested an exemption for some AI-generated advertising that was not intended to mislead, such as a virtual room used to present furniture. The discussion shows why a proportional approach is necessary. The more realistic a synthetic output is, and the more it can influence a customer decision, the stronger the disclosure and review should be.

Why this matters in Croatia

Croatian companies often use international AI services while remaining accountable within the European legal environment. Smaller organisations may not have separate legal, data, security and compliance departments. Simple standard rules are therefore more useful than a complex framework: every tool needs an owner, every sensitive use needs a review, every public output needs a disclosure decision and every supplier needs a basic documentation check.

Conclusion

Whether a particular date falls in August or December 2026 matters for formal planning, but not for strategy. Transparent content, human review, supplier documentation and reliable records are becoming ordinary elements of corporate management. The strongest organisations will be able to show how an output was created, who checked it and why the final decision was reasonable. That evidence trail turns AI from an uncontrolled experiment into dependable business infrastructure.
